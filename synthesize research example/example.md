## Output 2: Written Analysis

### What This Paper Argues

McKeon's central argument is that options-based portfolio management strategies have earned their place as permanent allocations within diversified investment portfolios. Writing in the immediate aftermath of the 2008 financial crisis, when the S&P 500 had suffered catastrophic losses and investor appetite for risk management had never been higher, McKeon contends that four specific strategies — buy-writes (BXM and BXY), put-writes (PUT), and collars (CLL) — offer a superior risk-return tradeoff compared to traditional long-only equity exposure. The timing is deliberate: when investors are most acutely aware of downside risk, the pitch for strategies that structurally reduce volatility is at its most compelling.

The mechanism McKeon proposes is straightforward and well-established in options theory. By systematically selling options against an equity position (or selling puts collateralized by cash), an investor collects premium income that partially offsets losses in down markets while accepting a cap on upside participation in strong rallies. The net effect, as measured by CBOE benchmark indices over twenty-plus years, is a return stream that delivers comparable or superior average returns with substantially lower volatility. McKeon's framing is not that these strategies are a free lunch, but rather that the risk-return tradeoff they offer — less upside in exchange for less downside and steady premium income — is one that most investors should rationally prefer to unhedged equity exposure, particularly after the painful lessons of 2008.

The data McKeon brings to bear is drawn entirely from CBOE-constructed benchmark indices: the BXM (at-the-money buy-write), BXY (2% out-of-the-money buy-write), PUT (cash-secured put-write), and CLL (95-110 collar). These indices use daily return data from 1988 through March 2009 and follow a rules-based methodology for rolling options positions at or near expiration. McKeon supplements his data presentation with references to third-party research from Ibbotson Associates, Callan Associates, and Ennis Knupp, though he does not reproduce or critically examine their findings.

### How They Make Their Case

The paper's evidentiary structure rests almost entirely on two tables of descriptive statistics comparing six return series — BXM, BXY, PUT, CLL, S&P 500, and the Barclays Aggregate Bond Index — over two overlapping periods. The first table covers 1988 through March 2009, incorporating the full impact of the 2008 crash. The second covers 1988 through December 2007, allowing the reader to see performance absent the crisis.

Over the full period through March 2009, three of the four OBPMS indices beat the S&P 500 in average annual return: the PUT index delivered 10.05% versus 7.68% for the S&P 500, the BXY delivered 9.29%, and the BXM delivered 8.76%. Only the CLL, at 6.89%, underperformed the equity benchmark. More striking than the return figures is the risk reduction. The PUT index achieved its superior return with a standard deviation of just 9.71%, compared to 14.77% for the S&P 500 — roughly one-third less volatility. The BXM showed a standard deviation of 10.44%, and even the BXY, with the least risk reduction among the buy-write variants, came in at 12.28%.

The Sharpe ratio comparisons are where McKeon's case looks strongest on the surface. The PUT index posted a Sharpe ratio of 0.5838, more than double the S&P 500's 0.2229. The BXM's Sharpe ratio of 0.4192 also comfortably exceeded the equity benchmark. These are meaningful differences over a twenty-year sample. McKeon notes, fairly, that the Aggregate Bond Index achieved an even higher Sharpe ratio of 0.7329, though he positions the OBPMS as equity-substitute strategies rather than bond competitors.

The up-market and down-market capture ratios provide additional texture. The PUT index captured only 59.8% of the S&P 500's upside but just 39.9% of its downside — an asymmetry that explains much of its superior risk-adjusted performance. The BXM showed a similar pattern with 64.5% up-capture and 54.5% down-capture. The CLL strategy, despite its explicit downside protection through long puts, actually captured 73.6% of down markets while only participating in 71.9% of up markets — a less favorable asymmetry that explains its weaker Sharpe ratio. McKeon does not explore why the collar underperforms on this metric, which would have been a valuable analytical contribution.

When the 2008 crisis year is excluded (the through-December-2007 table), all strategies look markedly better. The PUT index posted 12.50% annualized with 8.19% standard deviation and a Sharpe ratio of 0.9672 — an exceptional figure. The BXM delivered 11.41% (nearly identical to the S&P 500's 11.45%) with standard deviation of 9.05% versus 13.59%. McKeon presents this second table to show that the strategies' advantage is not merely a crisis artifact, but rather a persistent feature of their return profile.

Beyond the data tables, McKeon's argument proceeds through several qualitative claims. He asserts that these strategies are "highly investable," contrasting them favorably with hedge fund allocations that are "esoteric, opaque, illiquid, expensive and risky." He emphasizes the deep liquidity of the S&P 500 options market, citing over 346 million contracts traded in March 2009 alone. He argues that OBPMS should be formalized within an Investment Policy Statement rather than implemented as ad hoc overlays, because informal option overlays introduce timing risk and disrupt the formal asset allocation framework.

McKeon also briefly describes three additional strategies beyond the core four: the "1x2x3" (long stock, long 2 ATM calls, short 3 OTM calls for zero net premium), deep in-the-money LEAPS plus cash, and VIX exposure as a portfolio hedge. However, he acknowledges that return data for these strategies is limited and does not present performance statistics for them.

The paper explicitly cites supporting research from Ibbotson Associates (2004), Callan Associates, Ennis Knupp, and academic work by Kapadia, Szado, and Kazemi published through the Options Industry Council. McKeon characterizes these studies as "constructive" toward OBPMS, though he does not engage with any contrary findings or with the academic literature on why options may be priced to compensate for specific risks (such as crash risk or variance risk) that make the observed premium capture fair compensation rather than alpha.

One notable omission is any discussion of the economic source of the apparent free lunch. Finance theory holds that if options are fairly priced, the premium collected should, on average, compensate the seller for the risks assumed — particularly the tail risk of large losses. McKeon's presentation does not address why systematic option selling should deliver persistent excess risk-adjusted returns, which is the central question any sophisticated allocator would ask.

The paper also does not address transaction costs, bid-ask spreads in the options market, the impact of rolling positions at potentially disadvantageous times, or the tax treatment of options strategies — all of which are material to a real-world investor considering an allocation.

### Concrete Examples

Consider a pension fund with a $500 million equity allocation invested entirely in an S&P 500 index fund. Over the twenty-year period McKeon examines, this fund would have experienced annualized returns of 7.68% with standard deviation of 14.77%. The fund's investment committee, meeting in early 2009 after watching the portfolio lose roughly 37% of its value in 2008, is deeply concerned about meeting its assumed rate of return — typically around 7.5% to 8% for public pension funds during this era — while avoiding another catastrophic drawdown.

McKeon's paper would suggest that this pension fund consider reallocating a portion of its equity exposure to a PUT-write strategy. Had the fund instead invested in the PUT index, it would have earned 10.05% annualized — 237 basis points more per year — with standard deviation of just 9.71%, roughly two-thirds the volatility of the pure equity allocation. In practical terms, this means the fund's worst years would have been less severe, and the steady premium collection from selling puts would have contributed a consistent income stream that partially cushioned drawdowns.

To make this tangible: in a year where the S&P 500 fell 20%, the PUT index's down-market capture ratio of 39.9% suggests the strategy would have declined approximately 8%, meaning the fund would have preserved roughly $60 million more capital on that $500 million allocation compared to staying in pure equities. The tradeoff is that in a year where the S&P 500 rallied 20%, the PUT strategy's up-capture of 59.8% would have delivered only about 12% — the fund would have "left behind" roughly $40 million in gains. McKeon's implicit argument is that for a pension fund focused on meeting a liability stream, the certainty of smaller losses is worth more than the possibility of larger gains.

Now consider a high-net-worth individual managing a $10 million portfolio who seeks equity market exposure but has a low tolerance for drawdowns after the 2008 experience. This investor might look at the BXM (at-the-money buy-write) strategy, which delivered 8.76% annualized with 10.44% standard deviation. The investor would own the S&P 500 and systematically sell one-month at-the-money call options against the position. Each month, the premium collected — which during periods of elevated volatility like early 2009 could be 3% to 5% of the index value — would flow directly into the portfolio as income.

In a flat or modestly rising market, this strategy excels: the option expires worthless or only slightly in the money, and the investor keeps most or all of the premium. In a sharply rising month (say the S&P 500 jumps 8%), the investor's stock gains are capped near the strike price, and the option is called away — the premium helps, but the investor misses the bulk of the rally. The investor effectively trades away home-run months in exchange for a higher batting average. McKeon's data shows this tradeoff has been favorable on average over twenty years, with the BXM's Sharpe ratio of 0.4192 nearly doubling the S&P 500's 0.2229.

It is important to note, however, that McKeon's examples of the favorable risk-return tradeoff are drawn from a period that includes some of the most volatile markets in modern history — the 1990 recession, the 1998 LTCM crisis, the 2000–2002 dot-com bust, and the 2008 financial crisis. Periods of high realized volatility are precisely when systematic option selling is most profitable because premiums are elevated. An investor implementing these strategies during a sustained low-volatility bull market (such as 2012–2017) might find the premium income insufficient to compensate for the capped upside, producing a less favorable return profile than McKeon's historical data suggests.

### The Bottom Line

McKeon's paper serves as a useful, accessible introduction to options-based portfolio strategies for investors unfamiliar with them, and the historical return data from CBOE indices is legitimately interesting. However, this is fundamentally an advocacy piece from an asset manager, not a rigorous analysis. The core question it leaves unanswered — whether the observed risk-adjusted outperformance represents genuine alpha or fair compensation for selling insurance against market crashes — is precisely the question that matters most for allocation decisions. Investors considering these strategies should supplement McKeon's presentation with the academic literature on the variance risk premium, crash risk, and the well-documented pattern that option-selling strategies perform well on average but suffer catastrophic losses in tail events, which is exactly the risk the Sharpe ratio understates.

## Output 4: Thesis Assessment

### Strengths

**Real data over a meaningful time horizon.** The CBOE indices used are rules-based, publicly auditable benchmarks with more than twenty years of daily return data. This is not backtested or hypothetical performance — the indices follow a transparent, mechanical methodology for establishing and rolling options positions. Twenty years spanning multiple market cycles (the early 1990s recession, the dot-com bubble and bust, the 2008 financial crisis) provides a reasonably representative sample of market environments, though not a statistically rigorous one.

**Practically oriented framing.** McKeon correctly identifies the most important question for a real-world allocator: not whether options are theoretically interesting, but whether specific implementable strategies improve a portfolio's risk-return profile when included as a formal allocation. His emphasis on incorporating OBPMS into the Investment Policy Statement rather than using ad hoc overlays reflects sound institutional practice and addresses a genuine implementation challenge that many investors face.

**Appropriate benchmarking.** Comparing the OBPMS to both the S&P 500 and the Aggregate Bond Index provides useful context. The strategies clearly occupy a different risk-return space than either pure equities or pure fixed income, which is valuable information for asset allocation decisions.

**Honest presentation of the collar's underperformance.** Although McKeon does not analyze why the CLL collar strategy has less favorable risk-return characteristics than the other three OBPMS, he does present its data honestly rather than cherry-picking only the most flattering strategies. The collar's down-market capture of 73.6% (exceeding its up-market capture of 71.9%) is a genuinely surprising and informative result.

### Weaknesses

**Conflict of interest and advocacy tone.** The paper is authored by the chief investment officer of an asset management firm that offers options-based portfolio strategies. This does not invalidate the data, but it means the reader should expect — and does receive — a one-sided presentation. McKeon describes the return data as "compelling," states the strategies "dominate" on the efficient frontier, and asserts their results merit "serious attention." At no point does he present a countervailing argument or acknowledge scenarios where these strategies would underperform.

**No analysis of tail risk or maximum drawdown.** Standard deviation and Sharpe ratio are symmetric risk measures that treat upside and downside volatility identically. For strategies that systematically sell options — which have concave payoff profiles and are exposed to large losses during market crashes — these are precisely the wrong risk metrics. The paper does not report maximum drawdown, worst monthly or quarterly return, skewness, kurtosis, or any measure that would capture the tail-risk characteristics of these strategies. Given that the paper was written during the worst financial crisis in generations, this omission is particularly striking.

**No engagement with the variance risk premium literature.** Academic finance has extensively documented that options, particularly index puts, are systematically "expensive" relative to the realized volatility that materializes — a phenomenon known as the variance risk premium. This means that selling options has historically been profitable on average, but the premium compensates sellers for bearing crash risk (similar to how insurance companies earn a premium for bearing catastrophic loss risk). McKeon presents the premium capture as if it were an unambiguous benefit, without addressing whether it is fair compensation for a specific, identifiable risk. This is the single most important analytical gap in the paper.

**No transaction cost or implementation analysis.** The CBOE indices are hypothetical portfolios that assume frictionless execution at mid-market prices. Real-world implementation involves bid-ask spreads in the options market (which can be material, especially during stressed markets when the strategies are most needed), the operational complexity of monthly rolls, and potential slippage. McKeon's recommendation that investors access these strategies through managed vehicles implicitly acknowledges this challenge but does not quantify the drag.

**Sample period sensitivity.** Although twenty years is a reasonable history, the paper's own two tables demonstrate significant sensitivity to the inclusion or exclusion of a single year (2008). The PUT index's Sharpe ratio jumps from 0.5838 to 0.9672 when 2008 is removed. This sensitivity to a single observation in a sample of twenty suggests the statistics are not robust, and that the strategies' apparent superiority is at least partially attributable to the specific sequence of market events in this sample.

**Missing formal statistical testing.** With approximately 20 annual return observations, the standard errors on annualized return differences are large. The 237-basis-point spread between the PUT index (10.05%) and the S&P 500 (7.68%) may not be statistically significant at conventional thresholds. Without any test of significance, the reader cannot distinguish between a genuine structural advantage and sampling noise.

### Open Questions

**Is the variance risk premium the complete explanation?** If the observed outperformance is entirely attributable to the variance risk premium, then these strategies are not generating alpha — they are earning fair compensation for bearing tail risk. This has very different implications for portfolio allocation than McKeon's framing suggests. A systematic study decomposing the OBPMS return into equity beta, variance risk premium, and residual would substantially advance the analysis.

**How do these strategies perform in sustained low-volatility environments?** The sample period includes several high-volatility regimes where option premiums are elevated. In a prolonged low-volatility bull market, the premium collected may be small relative to the upside surrendered, potentially producing long stretches of underperformance versus unhedged equity. Understanding the regime-dependence of these strategies is critical for setting realistic expectations.

**What is the optimal allocation weight?** McKeon argues for a "permanent allocation" to OBPMS but provides no guidance on how much. A mean-variance optimization (which he references as feasible with Zephyr's tools) would produce dramatically different allocations depending on whether one uses the full-period or pre-crisis statistics, and whether one incorporates higher moments (skewness, kurtosis) into the objective function.

**How do active versions of these strategies compare to the passive indices?** McKeon mentions that "the active approach offers multiple sources of excess return" but provides no data. The distinction between passive mechanical rolling and active strike selection, timing, and position sizing is potentially large and merits separate analysis.

**What explains the collar's weak performance relative to the put-write?** Both strategies involve selling options against equity exposure, yet the PUT index dramatically outperforms the CLL on every metric. Understanding whether this is driven by the collar's long put cost, its particular strike selection (5% OTM put, 10% OTM call), or something else would be analytically valuable.
